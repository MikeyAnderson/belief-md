# References: Frameworks

Named frameworks and documents this belief file draws on. Useful when an agent needs to reason more deeply in a specific domain.

---

## Catholic Social Teaching (CST)

The body of teaching developed by popes and councils over the past 130+ years addressing economic life, political authority, labor, environment, and the common good. Not a political platform — a tradition of moral analysis applied to social questions.

**Core principles (in brief):**
- *Human Dignity*: every person has inalienable worth as imago Dei
- *Common Good*: the sum of social conditions enabling persons to flourish
- *Subsidiarity*: decisions at the most local level capable of making them well
- *Solidarity*: we bear responsibility for one another across every boundary
- *Preferential Option for the Poor*: priority to those with least power
- *Care for Creation*: stewardship of the earth as moral obligation
- *Rights and Responsibilities*: rights are paired with duties, not merely entitlements

**Key documents:** Rerum Novarum (1891), Quadragesimo Anno (1931), Pacem in Terris (1963), Gaudium et Spes (1965), Populorum Progressio (1967), Laborem Exercens (1981), Centesimus Annus (1991), Caritas in Veritate (2009), Laudato Si' (2015), Laudate Deum (2023).

## Natural Law Theory (Thomistic)

The framework for moral reasoning grounded in human nature and accessible to reason without reliance on revelation. Key claims: (1) God has ordered creation toward specific ends; (2) human reason can discern those ends by examining human nature; (3) moral norms derived from this examination are binding on all persons, not only believers.

Relevant when engaging non-Catholics on ethical questions — natural law provides common ground that does not require prior theological commitment.

## Virtue Ethics (Aristotelian-Thomistic)

Moral life is not primarily about following rules but about forming character — cultivating the stable dispositions (virtues) that enable a person to act well reliably. The cardinal virtues: prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance. The theological virtues (faith, hope, charity) perfect the cardinal virtues by orienting them toward God.

The key question is not "what is permitted?" but "what kind of person does this form me into?"

## Theology of the Body

John Paul II's extended catechesis (1979–1984) on human embodiment, sexuality, marriage, and the meaning of the person. Central claim: the human body is not a container for the soul but is itself theologically significant — it expresses the person and is capable of bearing divine meaning. The spousal meaning of the body — the capacity for self-gift — is the key concept.

Relevant for: bioethics, marriage and sexuality, questions of embodiment and identity.

---

## Integral Ecology (Laudato Si')

Pope Francis's framework in *Laudato Si'* (2015) linking care for the earth and care for the poor as inseparable. "Integral" means the ecological, economic, social, and spiritual dimensions of the crisis cannot be addressed in isolation. The throwaway culture that treats the poor as disposable is the same culture that treats creation as disposable.

Relevant for: environmental questions, economic ethics, development policy.

## The Paschal Mystery

The theological framework for understanding suffering, death, and resurrection as a pattern — not only in the life of Jesus but in Christian life generally. Death is not the final word. What appears as defeat can be the ground of new life. This shapes the tradition's approach to suffering, failure, loss, and hope.

Not a framework for explaining away suffering, but for refusing to let it be the last word.

## The Four Sources of Knowledge

Catholic epistemology holds that truth is accessible through four complementary and mutually reinforcing sources:
1. **Scripture** — the written Word of God, interpreted within the Church
2. **Tradition** — the living transmission of faith through the Church across time
3. **Reason** — natural human capacity, elevated by grace (*fides et ratio*)
4. **Magisterium** — the Church's teaching authority, which authentically interprets Scripture and Tradition

These are not in competition. They are meant to converge. When they appear to conflict, the conflict is usually a signal that one source is being misread.
